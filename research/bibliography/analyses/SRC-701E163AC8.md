## Relevance to the thesis
This textbook provides the canonical foundation for the tabular RL strategy family used in the thesis:
- agent–environment interaction and finite MDPs;
- value functions, Bellman equations and optimality;
- temporal-difference learning and tabular control;
- SARSA as on-policy TD control;
- Q-learning as off-policy TD control;
- planning and learning with tabular models;
- Dyna-Q as integration of direct RL, model learning and planning;
- Dyna-Q+ as a changing-environment extension that deliberately revisits long-untried actions;
- non-stationary tracking with recency-sensitive updates.

The book is therefore the common theoretical source that makes the thesis's five-strategy comparison interpretable as a **mechanism comparison**, not as five unrelated algorithms chosen because they happen to run in GridWorld.

## Q-learning: common foundation for Fixed and Adaptive Q-Learning
Q-learning is presented as off-policy TD control. Its update target uses the reward plus the discounted maximum estimated action value at the next state. The behavior policy determines which state-action pairs are actually sampled, but the TD target is greedy with respect to the current value estimates.

The thesis intentionally creates two deployment strategies from this same algorithmic foundation:
- **Fixed Q-Learning:** starts from a learned nominal checkpoint and disables learning during the evaluation period. It measures what happens when previously acquired values are retained but cannot adapt after change.
- **Adaptive Q-Learning:** starts from the matched nominal knowledge/configuration and continues Q-learning updates online. Its adaptation mechanism is ordinary continual TD updating, not a changepoint detector, reset mechanism or context oracle.

This distinction is thesis-specific experimental design. The book supports the learning-rule semantics; it does not itself claim that freezing/continuing Q-learning is a complete taxonomy of resilience.

## SARSA: on-policy continual TD control
The book presents SARSA as on-policy TD control. Its update uses the transition quintuple state, action, reward, next state and **the next action actually selected by the current behavior policy**. Consequently, under exploratory behavior the target includes the consequences of the policy's own future exploratory choices rather than taking a maximum over next-state actions as Q-learning does.

This is the key reason SARSA is a scientifically useful comparator rather than a duplicate Q-learning implementation. In the thesis, Adaptive Q-Learning and SARSA can receive the same observation/reward/action interface and similar tabular capacity while differing in the TD target and policy relationship:
- Q-learning: off-policy optimality target;
- SARSA: on-policy behavior target.

The cliff-walking example in Chapter 6 illustrates that this distinction can produce different learned behavior when exploration has real consequences. It should not be overgeneralized into a universal claim that SARSA is safer or more resilient; the thesis must measure its post-change behavior directly.

## Dyna-Q: learning plus a learned model plus planning
Chapter 8 develops the Dyna architecture as a unification of direct reinforcement learning, model learning and planning. In tabular Dyna-Q, each real interaction can update Q-values and the learned model; additional planning steps sample previously observed state-action experience from that model and apply Q-learning-style backups without consuming additional real environment transitions.

The thesis uses **Dyna-Q** to isolate the effect of this learned-model planning mechanism. Plain Dyna-Q should therefore be interpreted as more than “Q-learning with extra iterations”: it allocates computation to simulated experience generated from an empirical model learned from interaction.

Planning steps are an important algorithmic/resource parameter. They can improve propagation of new information but also add CPU work; they must be controlled/matched explicitly in fair comparisons and measured independently from the number of environment interactions.

## Dyna-Q+ and changing environments
The changing-maze discussion in Chapter 8 exposes a weakness of planning from a stale learned model: after the environment changes, an agent may keep planning from old transitions unless real exploration discovers that the model is no longer correct.

Dyna-Q+ adds directed re-exploration. For each state-action pair it tracks how long it has been since the action was tried in real interaction. Planning can include a bonus increasing with this elapsed time, making long-untried transitions temporarily more attractive to test. The book also describes the associated treatment of actions not yet tried from visited states by allowing them into planning with an initial zero-reward self-loop model.

This establishes the exact mechanism-level contrast required by the thesis:
- **Dyna-Q:** learned model + planning over experienced information;
- **Dyna-Q+:** Dyna planning + explicit pressure to re-test stale/untried actions and discover changes.

The exploration bonus is not free: it can incur additional exploratory cost. Therefore Dyna-Q+ should be evaluated on degradation, cumulative deficit, terminal performance and recovery rather than assumed superior by construction.

## Non-stationarity and recency
The second-edition material explicitly treats non-stationary estimation. Sample averages are appropriate when reward distributions are stationary, while constant step-size updates put greater weight on recent observations and progressively less weight on older observations.

For the thesis this supports a simple but important conceptual boundary:
- a constant/recency-sensitive update is a **continual adaptation mechanism**;
- it is not a changepoint detector;
- it does not automatically identify which transition/reward mechanism changed;
- it does not guarantee recovery under arbitrary non-stationarity.

## Mechanism crosswalk for the thesis

| User-facing strategy | Core mechanism | Model use | Policy/update relation | Explicit change-seeking mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed Q-Learning | Retain learned Q-values; no deployment update | No | Off-policy values, frozen at evaluation | No |
| Adaptive Q-Learning | Continual Q-learning updates | No | Off-policy TD | No |
| SARSA | Continual behavior-policy TD updates | No | On-policy TD | No |
| Dyna-Q | Real TD updates + learned-model planning | Learned empirical model | Q-learning-style planning/control | No dedicated recency bonus |
| Dyna-Q+ | Dyna planning + directed re-exploration | Learned empirical model | Q-learning-style planning/control | Yes: recency/untried-action exploration |

This crosswalk describes algorithmic mechanisms only. It does not predetermine which strategy will be more resilient under the thesis's uncertainty conditions.

## Critical distinctions for the experiment
- **Constant step size ≠ change detector.** It continuously discounts older evidence but does not declare when a regime changed.
- **Q-learning ≠ resilience guarantee.** It is a learning rule; post-shift recovery must be measured empirically.
- **SARSA ≠ automatically safer.** On-policy semantics can change behavior under exploration, but superiority depends on the environment and metric.
- **Planning ≠ additional environment data.** Dyna planning spends computation on model-generated experience; planning budget must be reported separately from real interaction budget.
- **Dyna-Q ≠ Dyna-Q+.** The latter adds a deliberate mechanism for revisiting stale/untried actions in changing environments.
- **Stationary convergence theory ≠ non-stationary tracking guarantee.** Classical convergence assumptions do not justify optimal tracking under persistent or repeated changes.
- **Recency ≠ reset.** A constant step size retains prior estimates with decaying influence; a reset discards them explicitly.
