# Evidence — A Survey of Constraint Formulations in Safe Reinforcement Learning

## E1 — The safety formulation defines feasibility before the optimization algorithm is chosen
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Abstract; Sections 1–2; Figure 1
- **Claim:** Safe-RL problems based on constrained criteria consist of a problem-formulation step that defines the feasible policy space and a policy-optimization step that solves the resulting constrained problem.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
Wachi et al. emphasize that constrained safe RL cannot be characterized by the optimization algorithm alone. Different mathematical representations of safety produce different constraint functions and therefore different feasible policy sets. The formulation is logically prior to choosing or developing an algorithm suitable for that problem.

### Thesis use
For every safety baseline, state the constraint function, threshold, horizon/discount assumptions, and guarantee type before reporting performance.

### Citation
Wachi et al. (2024), Abstract, Sections 1–2, and Figure 1.

## E2 — Expected cumulative cost is an expectation-level budget
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Section 3.1
- **Claim:** A common CMDP formulation constrains the expected discounted cumulative safety cost below a threshold.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
The expected-cumulative formulation gives the safety cost a value-function structure analogous to reward and requires its expectation under the policy to remain below a specified bound. This structure is convenient for methods such as Lagrangian optimization because reward and safety value functions can be combined algebraically.

### Context and limits
An expectation-level budget does not guarantee that every trajectory is violation-free. Rare serious violations can still occur while the expected constraint is satisfied.

### Thesis use
Report empirical violation counts and probabilities in addition to expected cumulative safety cost.

### Citation
Wachi et al. (2024), Section 3.1.

## E3 — Expected unsafe-state visits and probability of any violation are different requirements
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Sections 3.2–3.3
- **Claim:** A state-constraint formulation can bound the expected accumulated number of unsafe-state visits, whereas a joint chance constraint bounds the probability that an unsafe state is visited at least once over the horizon.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
The survey separates an additive unsafe-state indicator constraint from a joint chance constraint. The former accumulates expected unsafe-state occupancy over time. The latter asks directly for the probability of the event that at least one unsafe state is encountered during the episode. Although both refer to the same unsafe set, they define different feasible policy spaces.

### Thesis use
Do not report an unsafe-state count as if it were a chance constraint; label the quantity actually constrained and measured.

### Citation
Wachi et al. (2024), Sections 3.2–3.3.

## E4 — Additive approximations to joint chance constraints can be conservative
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Section 3.3; relation to Problem 2
- **Claim:** The probability of any unsafe visit can be upper-bounded by the expected sum of unsafe-state indicators, but satisfying the additive bound may exclude policies that would satisfy the original chance constraint.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
Because direct joint chance constraints are difficult to optimize, prior work sometimes replaces them with an additive upper bound based on unsafe-state indicators. This transformation can make the problem tractable, but the surrogate may be stricter than the original probability requirement and therefore introduce additional conservatism.

### Thesis use
If a tractable surrogate is used for safety, state that it is a conservative approximation and report its relationship to the desired safety event.

### Citation
Wachi et al. (2024), Section 3.3.

## E5 — Instantaneous and almost-sure constraints are stronger notions than average cumulative constraints
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Sections 3.4–3.5
- **Claim:** Instantaneous constraints apply at every time step, while almost-sure cumulative constraints require the cumulative condition to hold with probability one.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
The survey presents formulations that impose an expected safety threshold at every individual decision step and others that require a cumulative safety condition to hold almost surely. These requirements are qualitatively stronger than constraining only an expected total cost over the episode.

### Context and limits
Stronger formulations often require stronger assumptions or more restrictive algorithms; they should not be claimed merely because empirical violations were rare.

### Thesis use
Place the declared guarantee type next to every safety result: expected, chance-constrained, instantaneous, almost-sure, empirical-only, or another explicitly defined category.

### Citation
Wachi et al. (2024), Sections 3.4–3.5.

## E6 — Transformability and conservative approximation do not imply algorithmic equivalence
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** theoretical-relations sections and conclusion
- **Claim:** Some safe-RL formulations can be transformed into or conservatively approximated by other formulations under stated assumptions, but their semantics and feasible sets remain distinct.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
Wachi et al. introduce formal notions such as transformability, generalizability, and conservative approximation to explain relationships among constraint formulations. A mathematical relationship between two formulations does not mean that they express the same safety requirement under all settings or that algorithms designed for one can be compared without accounting for the transformation assumptions and conservatism.

### Thesis use
Treat formulation conversion as part of the method specification rather than silently comparing methods under mismatched safety semantics.

### Citation
Wachi et al. (2024), theoretical relations and conclusion.
