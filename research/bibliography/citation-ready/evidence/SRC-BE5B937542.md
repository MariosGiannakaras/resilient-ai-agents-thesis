# Evidence — A Survey of Safe Reinforcement Learning and Constrained MDPs

## E1 — CMDPs keep task reward and safety costs separate
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Sections 3.1–3.2; Equation 1
- **Claim:** A constrained MDP maximizes expected reward while requiring one or more expected cumulative safety costs to remain below explicit thresholds.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
The survey defines a CMDP by augmenting the ordinary MDP with cost functions that encode unsafe behavior or constrained resource use. A policy remains feasible only when the expected discounted return of each cost function is no greater than its specified bound, while the primary objective remains maximizing task reward.

### Context and limits
An expectation constraint does not guarantee zero violations on every trajectory. The exact safety semantics depend on the chosen cost and constraint type.

### Thesis use
Report task return, cumulative safety cost, violation count, and violation severity separately.

### Citation
Kushwaha et al. (2026), Sections 3.1–3.2.

## E2 — Lagrangian methods optimize a penalized objective but do not make approximate training an exact guarantee
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Section 3.2; Section 4.1
- **Claim:** Lagrange multipliers transform CMDP constraints into a reward–cost trade-off that can be optimized with RL, while practical constraint satisfaction still depends on the optimization procedure.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
For fixed nonnegative multipliers, the CMDP Lagrangian is equivalent to an unconstrained objective with a reward penalized by weighted safety costs. Primal–dual approaches update both the policy and the multipliers so that violated constraints receive greater weight. This provides a practical route to constrained optimization but does not turn function approximation, finite data, or imperfect convergence into a per-trajectory safety guarantee.

### Thesis use
If a Lagrangian baseline is used, log multiplier trajectories and empirical constraint violations rather than inferring safety from the objective alone.

### Citation
Kushwaha et al. (2026), Sections 3.2 and 4.1.

## E3 — Safety shields are external intervention mechanisms
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Section 4.2, Safety Shields and Action Correction
- **Claim:** A runtime shield can reject or replace a proposed action when it is predicted to violate a safety specification.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
Shielding methods place a safety mechanism between the learned policy and the environment. The mechanism evaluates the proposed action and can override it with a safe alternative when a violation is predicted. The executed behavior therefore reflects both the raw policy and the intervention layer.

### Context and limits
A shield is not a change detector or an adaptation mechanism. Its effectiveness depends on the correctness and coverage of the monitor, model, or formal specification used for intervention.

### Thesis use
Report raw-policy behavior, shielded behavior, intervention count, intervention duration, and utility loss separately.

### Citation
Kushwaha et al. (2026), Section 4.2.

## E4 — Constrained safety, robust RL, and risk-sensitive RL solve different optimization problems
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Sections 1–2 and 4.3
- **Claim:** Explicit constraints, worst-case robustness, and risk-sensitive criteria are related but distinct formulations.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
The survey distinguishes CMDP-style safety constraints from robust objectives that optimize against model uncertainty or adverse disturbances and from risk-sensitive objectives that reshape preferences over the outcome distribution. These formulations can share optimization tools, but their guarantees and interpretations are different.

### Thesis use
Do not use “safe,” “robust,” and “resilient” interchangeably; declare the mathematical formulation associated with every safety-oriented baseline.

### Citation
Kushwaha et al. (2026), Sections 1–2 and 4.3.

## E5 — Non-stationarity can invalidate nominal safety assumptions
- **Type:** faithful paraphrase
- **Location:** Section 6, open research problem on non-stationarity
- **Claim:** Safety mechanisms designed for a fixed environment may need new assumptions or adaptation when dynamics, constraints, or other operating conditions change.
- **Status:** verified

### Faithful paraphrase
The survey identifies non-stationarity as an open challenge for safe RL. When the environment or its relevant safety conditions change, guarantees established for the original problem may no longer describe the deployed system, and agents may need mechanisms that recognize or adapt to changing conditions.

### Context and limits
This observation does not provide a complete resilience algorithm or empirical ranking for non-stationary safe RL.

### Thesis use
Evaluate safety costs around environmental changes and keep safety recovery metrics distinct from task-return recovery.

### Citation
Kushwaha et al. (2026), Section 6.
